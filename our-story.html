Twenty years, one kitchen

We spent two decades making treats that went out under other companies' names. This is why that changed.

In 2005 my father bought a second-hand smoker and started selling to six pet shops within an hour's drive. He had no marketing budget and no interest in getting one. What he had was a rule: if he wouldn't give it to his own dog, it didn't leave the building.

That rule turned out to be a decent business plan. Six shops became sixty, then a few hundred. Most of them wanted the treats under their own label, and we said yes every time — that's how a small manufacturer pays for itself. For twenty years, if you bought a single-ingredient chew from an independent pet shop in this part of the country, there is a fair chance it came out of our kitchen with somebody else's sticker on it.

He never wanted his name on the bag. I think it should be.

What changed

I grew up in that kitchen — weekends, summers, and eventually properly. I learned which batches he'd reject and why. I learned that the difference between a chew that lasts ten minutes and one that lasts an hour is mostly patience, and that almost nobody is patient because patience is expensive.

When I took over, I didn't want to change a single recipe. There was nothing wrong with them. What was wrong was that you couldn't buy them unless you happened to walk into the right shop, and even then you'd pay a mark-up to somebody who hadn't made anything.

What didn't change

Same kitchen. Same smoker, actually — he won't let me replace it. Same suppliers, most of whom we've used for over a decade and can name. Same rule about what leaves the building.

We still supply the trade, and we always will — those shops built this company and we're not walking away from them. But now you can also just buy the treats directly, in a bag with our name on it, for less than you'd pay for the same thing with somebody else's.

The name

Chew Cartel started as a joke between us about being the supplier nobody was allowed to mention. It stuck. The badge on the bag is the one my father drew on the back of an invoice about fifteen years ago, cleaned up but not much.
