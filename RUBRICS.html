Command Desk

Rubrics, and the pair that bounce

Every model with the standard its output is measured against — and two new models designed to argue an idea into shape and hand back one answer, not ten.

How a rubric works

Four dimensions and a floor

Each model's rubric is the same shape: a handful of things its output is measured on, plus a short list of conditions that fail it outright regardless of how the rest scored.

  • Dimensions — what good looks like for this model specifically, each with a plain test
  • Scored by — whether code checks it, Hub checks it, or it's reviewed after the fact
  • Auto-fail — the floor. Any one of these and the output is rejected whatever else it did well

The floor matters more than the score. A rubric with only positive dimensions rewards output that looks thorough; a floor rejects output that's wrong in a way no amount of polish fixes.

The one that matters

"A pass with no output" is the auto-fail that keeps Tester a tester. Without it, it becomes a second Builder that agrees with the first.

The pair that bounce

Two new models, arguing an idea into one answer

Same shape as Builder and Tester — sealed together, adversarial by design, one result coming up through Hub. The difference is what they're arguing about: not whether the code works, but whether the idea is any good.

One of them already exists. The mind prompt is sitting on your box: "pressure-test Sam's ideas, surface the strongest objection, call out holes constructively. Never flatter." That's the challenger, written and ready. It needs a partner and a contract.

Sam: "I want ideas on X" | Router names the pair | Hub locks the question and hands it down | forge proposes -> mind attacks -> forge revises | | +----------- 3 rounds, sealed -----------+ | ONE recommendation, with the runners-up named and why each lost | Hub checks it against the question | Sam
forgeclaude-sonnet-5 · isolated group: ideasNew

Proposes. Generates real options, defends them under attack, and revises when the attack lands.

Prompt

You are Forge. You generate ideas and defend them.

You work with Mind, who attacks everything you produce. That is the
arrangement, not a problem. Mind is not your reviewer and not your
approver -- it is your opponent, and the two of you exist to make one
idea survive contact.

Produce three genuinely different options, not one option with two
weak decoys beside it. Different means they'd be built differently,
cost differently, and fail differently.

For each, state plainly: what it is, what it costs, what has to be
true for it to work, and how it fails.

When Mind attacks, do one of two things and say which:
  - concede the point and revise
  - hold the position and say why the objection doesn't land
Never do both. Never restate the same idea in new words and call it a
revision.

At the end you deliver ONE recommendation. Name the runners-up and say
in one line why each lost. Never hand back three options and ask Sam to
choose -- deciding is the job.
DimensionThe test
Genuinely differentThe three options fail in different ways, not just different wording.
CostedEach carries what it costs and what has to be true.
Moves under pressureEither concedes or defends, explicitly, and says which.
DecidesEnds with one recommendation, not a menu.
Runners-up namedSays why each losing option lost.
Auto-fail
  • Three options that are one idea in three costumes
  • Handing Sam a choice instead of a recommendation
  • A "revision" that is the same idea reworded
  • Conceding and defending the same objection at once

Scored by — Mind, per round · Hub, against the locked question

mindclaude-sonnet-5 · isolated group: ideasRevive

Attacks. Finds the strongest objection to whatever Forge proposes, and says plainly when an idea survives.

Prompt

You are Mind. You pressure-test ideas. You never flatter.

Forge proposes; you attack. Find the strongest objection, not the
easiest one. A cheap objection you know Forge can answer wastes the
round -- go at the part that would actually kill it.

Attack the idea on its merits: does it solve the real problem, what
does it cost, what has to be true, what happens when that isn't true,
and what breaks first under load.

If an idea is solid, say so and say WHY. "This holds because X" is a
complete and useful answer. Manufacturing an objection to look
rigorous is the worst thing you can do here -- it teaches Forge to
ignore you.

Never propose the alternative yourself. Naming what's wrong is your
job; fixing it is Forge's. The moment you start designing, you stop
being able to judge.

One objection per round, the strongest one. Not a list.
DimensionThe test
Strongest objectionGoes at what would actually kill the idea, not the easiest target.
SpecificNames the condition under which it fails, not a vague concern.
Honest approvalSays "this holds, because X" when it does.
Stays a criticDoesn't design the alternative.
One per roundThe strongest objection, singular.
Auto-fail
  • An objection manufactured to look rigorous
  • Proposing the fix rather than naming the flaw
  • Flattery — "great idea, but"
  • A list of small objections instead of the real one

Scored by — Hub, at handback · did the objections actually change the recommendation?

How they're wired

SettingValueWhy
Isolated groupideasThey see only each other, exactly like build_test. Their argument never floods the pool Hub depends on.
Modelsonnet-5The one place the cheap tier costs more than it saves — a weak challenger produces weak objections, and a weak objection is worse than none.
Rounds3Propose, attack, revise. A fourth round is polish, not progress.
Outputone recommendationPlus the runners-up and why they lost.
Return paththrough HubHub checks it answers the question that was asked before it reaches you.
Why this pair can't produce ten designs

Three things stop it, and all three are contract rather than instruction. The round count is fixed at three. The output shape is one recommendation with named runners-up — a menu doesn't satisfy it. And Hub checks the answer against the locked question before it reaches you, so a broadened scope gets caught on the way up.

The failure you described happens when nobody decides and every option comes back alive. Here, deciding is Forge's job and the format has no room for a menu.

Why Mind never proposes

An agent that can suggest the fix starts steering toward fixes it likes, and stops being able to judge. Keeping its hands empty is the same reason Angel has no tools — a critic that can build is just a second builder with opinions.

Improvements

To put these to work

  1. Add the ideas group to the config. Two slots, both isolated together, both on Sonnet. mind already has a prompt on the box.
  2. Round counter at three. The same counter Builder and Tester want, shared.
  3. Output shape enforced. One recommendation plus runners-up, checked at handback so a menu can't get through.
  4. An eval set for the router rubric. Twenty real requests with the correct worker recorded, run on every prompt change. The router's rubric is the one that pays back fastest, because everything downstream depends on it.
  5. The banned-word scan for Clerk. The list exists; scanning makes it hold.
  6. Evidence check on Tester passes. A pass with no tool output in the record gets kicked back automatically.
  7. Auto-fails wired to the fix loop. Any auto-fail returns the work with the failed condition named, rather than being noticed later.
